\title{EvoLearning: Evolutionary Expert-Guided Reinforcement Learning for Personalized Learning Path Recommendation}

\author{Anonymous}
\affiliation{\institution{Anonymous Institution} \country{}}
\email{anonymous@anonymous.org}

\begin{abstract}
Personalized learning path recommendation (LPR) aims to identify an optimal sequence of knowledge concepts that maximizes a learner's mastery improvement. Reinforcement learning (RL) approaches interact with knowledge evolution simulators to learn recommendation policies, but face a fundamental \emph{search space explosion}: for $M$ concepts and path length $L$, the space of possible paths grows combinatorially, reaching $10^{38}$ for typical curricula with $L=20$. Randomly initialized policies cannot efficiently explore this space under sparse rewards. We propose \ours{}, a three-stage framework that tames this combinatorial challenge through \emph{evolutionary pre-exploration}. First, an evolutionary algorithm with diversity-regularized selection generates high-quality, diverse expert trajectories for each learner. Second, these trajectories are distilled into a neural policy via behavioral cloning (BC), compressing per-instance search results into a generalizable model. Third, proximal policy optimization (PPO) fine-tunes the policy through online interaction, discovering improvements beyond the evolutionary ceiling. Experiments on two real-world datasets (ASSISTments 2009 and Junyi Academy) with 10 baselines demonstrate that \ours{} achieves state-of-the-art effectiveness percentage (EP) scores and converges up to $7\times$ faster than vanilla PPO.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%% ============================================================

Intelligent tutoring systems increasingly rely on personalized learning path recommendation (LPR) to guide learners through a curriculum of knowledge concepts (KCs) in an order that maximizes their mastery development~\cite{liu2019cseal,li2023gehrl,cheng2025knowlp}. Given a learner's current knowledge state and a set of target concepts, the goal is to recommend a sequence of $L$ concepts to practice such that the learner's proficiency on the targets improves as much as possible.

Recent advances frame LPR as a sequential decision-making problem and apply reinforcement learning (RL), where a knowledge evolution simulator (KES)---typically a deep knowledge tracing (DKT) model~\cite{piech2015dkt}---serves as the environment~\cite{liu2019cseal}. The agent receives the learner's mastery state, selects concepts to recommend, and is rewarded based on the resulting mastery improvement. Methods such as CSEAL~\cite{liu2019cseal}, SRC~\cite{wang2023src}, GEHRL~\cite{li2023gehrl}, and DLELP/KnowLP~\cite{cheng2025knowlp} have demonstrated the viability of this paradigm.

However, existing RL-based LPR methods face a fundamental challenge: the \textbf{search space explosion} in long-horizon recommendations. For a curriculum of $M$ knowledge concepts and path length $L$, the number of distinct paths grows as $\binom{M}{L} \cdot L!$---for $M=123$ and $L=20$, this exceeds $10^{30}$. A randomly initialized RL policy must navigate this vast space with only sparse terminal rewards, leading to prohibitively slow convergence. This problem is particularly acute in practical settings where longer paths ($L \geq 10$) are pedagogically necessary but exponentially harder to optimize.

We propose \ours{}, a three-stage framework that addresses the search space challenge through \emph{evolutionary pre-exploration}: rather than asking a neural policy to search from scratch, we first use evolution to identify high-quality regions of the path space, then teach the policy to operate within these regions via imitation, and finally refine through online RL.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Evolutionary Expert Generation.} For each training learner, an evolutionary algorithm efficiently searches the path space within the KES. A diversity-regularized greedy selection inspired by determinantal point processes (DPP)~\cite{kulesza2012dpp} yields $K$ diverse expert trajectories per learner, ensuring broad coverage of effective strategies.

    \item \textbf{Behavioral Cloning Warm-Start.} The expert trajectories are distilled into a neural policy via supervised learning. This compresses the per-instance evolutionary knowledge into a \emph{generalizable} mapping from learner states to concept recommendations, effectively reducing the search space from $\binom{M}{L}$ to the neighborhood of demonstrated strategies.

    \item \textbf{PPO Fine-Tuning.} Starting from the BC-initialized policy, PPO refines the strategy through online interaction with the KES. Because the policy already operates in a high-reward region, PPO's exploration is directed toward local improvements rather than global random search.
\end{enumerate}

Our main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We introduce \ours{}, a novel LPR framework that bridges evolutionary search and policy optimization through behavioral cloning, achieving state-of-the-art performance on standard benchmarks.
    \item We propose a DPP-based diverse expert selection mechanism that prevents mode collapse in evolutionary trajectories and improves the quality of the BC initialization.
    \item We conduct comprehensive experiments comparing 10 methods across 2 datasets, demonstrating that \ours{} significantly outperforms all baselines and converges up to $7\times$ faster than vanilla PPO.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Knowledge Tracing}

Knowledge tracing (KT) models estimate a learner's evolving mastery over time from interaction sequences. Deep Knowledge Tracing (DKT)~\cite{piech2015dkt} pioneered the use of recurrent neural networks for this task. Subsequent models include DKVMN~\cite{zhang2017dkvmn}, SAKT~\cite{pandey2019sakt}, AKT~\cite{ghosh2020akt}, and SAINT~\cite{choi2020saint}, which leverage attention mechanisms and memory networks for improved accuracy. In LPR, KT models serve dual roles: as the knowledge evolution simulator (KES) for RL training and as the mastery estimator at inference time.

\subsection{Learning Path Recommendation}

Early LPR methods relied on heuristic rules such as prerequisite ordering~\cite{chen2018prerequisite} and difficulty matching~\cite{liu2019cseal}. The CSEAL framework~\cite{liu2019cseal} introduced the KES paradigm, training an RL agent within a DKT simulator using actor-critic methods with cognitive navigation---restricting the action space to graph-related concepts. SRC~\cite{wang2023src} formulated LPR as a set-to-sequence problem with concept-aware ranking. GEHRL~\cite{li2023gehrl} proposed hierarchical RL with a goal-planning agent and a goal-achieving agent, incorporating graph-based candidate filtering. DLELP and KnowLP~\cite{cheng2025knowlp} employ a multi-agent system with prerequisite and similarity agents, using EDU-GraphRAG for knowledge graph construction. GRU4Rec~\cite{hidasi2016gru4rec}, originally designed for session-based recommendation, has been adapted for sequential learning path generation.

\subsection{Bridging Search and Policy Optimization}

The paradigm of initializing RL with demonstrations has been studied extensively in robotics. Rajeswaran et al.~\cite{rajeswaran2018dexterous} use human demonstrations to warm-start natural policy gradient for dexterous manipulation, and Nair et al.~\cite{nair2018demonstrations} augment replay buffers with demonstrations to overcome exploration in sparse-reward tasks. These works rely on \emph{pre-existing expert demonstrations} (e.g., human teleoperation), which are expensive to collect and domain-specific.

Our approach differs in a fundamental way: we \emph{generate} demonstrations automatically via evolutionary search, eliminating the need for human experts. This is made possible by a key property of the LPR domain: the KES provides a \emph{zero-cost, deterministic simulator}. Evaluating a candidate path requires only $L$ forward passes through a small LSTM, making per-instance evolutionary search tractable---each learner's optimal path can be approximated through 60 candidates over 50 generations in under one second. In contrast, robotics domains require expensive physical simulation, making per-instance search infeasible.

Hybrid evolutionary-gradient methods such as ERL~\cite{khadka2018erl} and population-based training~\cite{jaderberg2017pbt} integrate evolution into the RL training loop, but evolve the \emph{policy parameters} rather than generating per-instance demonstrations. Evolutionary strategies~\cite{salimans2017es} replace gradient descent entirely. Our approach occupies a different point in this design space: evolution operates at the \emph{solution level} (generating paths for individual learners), while gradient-based learning operates at the \emph{policy level} (generalizing across learners). This separation allows each component to operate in its natural regime.

\section{Preliminaries}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

Let $\mathcal{K} = \{k_1, k_2, \ldots, k_M\}$ denote a set of $M$ knowledge concepts in a curriculum. A learner is characterized by their interaction history $\mathcal{H} = \{(c_1, r_1), (c_2, r_2), \ldots\}$ where $c_t \in \mathcal{K}$ is the practiced concept and $r_t \in \{0, 1\}$ is the binary correctness outcome at step $t$.

\begin{definition}[Learning Path Recommendation]
Given a learner's history $\mathcal{H}$, a set of target concepts $\mathcal{G} \subseteq \mathcal{K}$, and a path length $L$, the goal is to find an ordered sequence $\mathbf{p} = (p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_L) \in \mathcal{K}^L$ that maximizes the learner's mastery improvement on the targets $\mathcal{G}$.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Knowledge Evolution Simulator (KES)}

Following the CSEAL framework~\cite{liu2019cseal}, we employ a trained DKT model~\cite{piech2015dkt} as the KES. Given a history $\mathcal{H}$, the DKT produces a mastery vector $\mathbf{h} \in [0,1]^M$ where $h_i$ represents the estimated probability of correctly answering concept $k_i$.

The KES operates with a deterministic response model: when a learner practices concept $c$, the outcome is determined by
\begin{equation}
    r = \mathbf{1}[h_c > 0.5]
\end{equation}
where $h_c$ is the learner's current mastery of concept $c$. The interaction $(c, r)$ is appended to $\mathcal{H}$, and the DKT produces an updated mastery vector.

\subsection{Effectiveness Percentage (EP)}

The standard evaluation metric for LPR is the Effectiveness Percentage~\cite{liu2019cseal}, which measures the normalized mastery improvement on target concepts after executing a learning path:
\begin{equation}
    \ep = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}'|} \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}'} \frac{E_g^{\text{end}} - E_g^{\text{start}}}{1 - E_g^{\text{start}}}
    \label{eq:ep}
\end{equation}
where $E_g^{\text{start}}$ and $E_g^{\text{end}}$ are the learner's mastery of target $g$ before and after the path, and $\mathcal{G}' = \{g \in \mathcal{G} : 1 - E_g^{\text{start}} > 0.01\}$ excludes near-mastered targets.

\subsection{Concept Prerequisite Graph}

We construct a concept graph $\mathcal{G}_c = (\mathcal{K}, \mathcal{E}_p, \mathcal{E}_s)$ where $\mathcal{E}_p$ denotes prerequisite edges and $\mathcal{E}_s$ denotes similarity edges. Following prior work~\cite{liu2019cseal,cheng2025knowlp}, we derive these from the DKT's cross-concept influence: for each concept $k_i$, we measure the change in mastery of all other concepts when $k_i$ is practiced correctly. An edge $(k_i, k_j) \in \mathcal{E}_p$ if the influence exceeds a threshold $\tau$.

\section{Method: \ours{}}
%% ============================================================

\ours{} consists of three stages, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:framework}. We describe each stage and the overall training pipeline.

\subsection{Stage 1: Evolutionary Expert Generation}

For each training learner with history $\mathcal{H}$, targets $\mathcal{G}$, and initial mastery $\mathbf{h}_0$, we search for high-quality learning paths using an evolutionary algorithm.

\paragraph{Candidate Space.} We construct a candidate set $\mathcal{C}$ of size at most $C$ by combining the target concepts, their prerequisites and similar concepts from the concept graph, and zone-of-proximal-development (ZPD) concepts whose mastery is closest to 0.5:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{C} = \mathcal{G} \cup \bigcup_{g \in \mathcal{G}} \left(\text{prereq}(g) \cup \text{sim}_K(g)\right) \cup \text{ZPD}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Evolutionary Search.} We maintain a population of $N_{\text{pop}}$ candidate paths, each of length $L$ drawn from $\mathcal{C}$. Over $N_{\text{gen}}$ generations, the population evolves through tournament selection, ordered crossover, and point mutation. The fitness of each path $\mathbf{p}$ is evaluated in the KES:
\begin{equation}
    f(\mathbf{p}) = \ep(\mathcal{H}, \mathbf{p}, \mathcal{G})
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Diversity-Regularized Expert Selection.} To prevent mode collapse in the expert set, we select $K$ diverse paths per learner using a greedy DPP-inspired procedure. Starting with the highest-fitness path, we iteratively add the path that maximizes:
\begin{equation}
    \text{score}(\mathbf{p}) = f(\mathbf{p}) - \lambda \cdot \max_{\mathbf{p}' \in \mathcal{S}} \text{sim}(\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{p}')
    \label{eq:dpp}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{S}$ is the currently selected set, $\text{sim}(\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{p}') = |\mathbf{p} \cap \mathbf{p}'| / L$ measures concept overlap, and $\lambda$ controls the quality--diversity trade-off. This greedy procedure mirrors the sequential selection in DPP MAP inference~\cite{kulesza2012dpp}, where the log-determinant objective decomposes into a quality term minus a redundancy penalty. While we use a simplified Jaccard-based similarity kernel rather than a full DPP kernel matrix, the objective structure---balancing item quality against pairwise redundancy---is equivalent.

\subsection{Stage 2: Behavioral Cloning}

The evolutionary expert trajectories provide state--action pairs for supervised learning. For each expert $(\mathbf{h}_0, \mathcal{G}, \mathbf{p}, \text{EP})$, we construct $L$ training examples:
\begin{equation}
    \{(\mathbf{s}_t, a_t)\}_{t=0}^{L-1}, \quad \mathbf{s}_t = [\mathbf{h}_0 \| \mathbf{g} \| t/L]
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{g} \in \{0,1\}^M$ is the target mask and $a_t = p_t$ is the expert's action at step $t$.

\paragraph{State design note.} The BC state uses the \emph{initial} mastery $\mathbf{h}_0$ (fixed across all steps), whereas PPO uses the \emph{current} mastery $\mathbf{h}_t$ (updated after each KES interaction). This simplification avoids $L$ KES calls per expert per BC epoch, which would be prohibitively expensive for large expert sets. The step index $t/L$ provides the policy with positional information to compensate for the static mastery. Crucially, the PPO stage trains on the policy's own state distribution with live KES feedback, correcting any mismatch introduced by this approximation.

The policy $\pi_\theta$ is trained to minimize the cross-entropy loss:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\bc} = -\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|} \sum_{(\mathbf{s}, a) \in \mathcal{D}} \log \pi_\theta(a \mid \mathbf{s})
\end{equation}

\subsection{Stage 3: PPO Fine-Tuning}

Starting from the BC-initialized policy, we apply PPO~\cite{schulman2017ppo} for online fine-tuning. At each episode, we sample a batch of learners and collect trajectories by interacting with the KES.

\paragraph{State and Action.} The state at step $t$ is $\mathbf{s}_t = [\mathbf{h}_t \| \mathbf{g} \| t/L] \in \R^{2M+1}$ where $\mathbf{h}_t$ is the current mastery vector. The action $a_t \in \mathcal{K} \setminus \{a_0, \ldots, a_{t-1}\}$ selects a concept not previously recommended in the current path.

\paragraph{Reward.} We use a sparse terminal reward:
\begin{equation}
    R_t = \begin{cases} \ep(\mathcal{H}, \mathbf{p}_{0:L}, \mathcal{G}) & \text{if } t = L-1 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Policy Optimization.} The PPO clipped surrogate objective is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\ppo} = -\E_t\left[\min\left(\rho_t \hat{A}_t,\ \text{clip}(\rho_t, 1\pm\epsilon) \hat{A}_t\right)\right]
\end{equation}
where $\rho_t = \pi_\theta(a_t|\mathbf{s}_t) / \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t|\mathbf{s}_t)$ is the importance ratio, $\hat{A}_t$ is the advantage estimate, and $\epsilon$ is the clipping parameter. The full loss includes a value function term and an entropy bonus:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\ppo} + c_v \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{value}} - c_e \cdot H[\pi_\theta]
\end{equation}

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Datasets}

We evaluate on two widely-used educational datasets:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ASSISTments 2009}~\cite{feng2009assistments}: An online mathematics tutoring platform. After preprocessing, the dataset contains 2,303 learners, 123 knowledge concepts, and 338K interactions.
    \item \textbf{Junyi Academy}~\cite{chang2015junyi}: A Chinese mathematics learning platform. We sample 10,000 learners (seed-controlled), yielding 39 knowledge concepts and 2.4M interactions.
\end{itemize}

Following the CSEAL protocol~\cite{liu2019cseal}, we split each dataset 50/50 into $\mathcal{D}_{\text{Sim}}$ (for DKT training) and $\mathcal{D}_{\text{Off}}$ (for RL training and evaluation). $\mathcal{D}_{\text{Off}}$ is further split 80/10/10 into training, validation, and test sets.

\subsection{Baselines}

We compare against 9 methods spanning 5 categories:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Heuristic:} \textit{Random} (uniform concept sampling), \textit{Target-repeat} (cyclic repetition of target concepts), \textit{Rule-based} (topological sort of prerequisite graph).
    \item \textbf{Sequential:} \textit{GRU4Rec}~\cite{hidasi2016gru4rec} (GRU-based autoregressive model trained on expert trajectories via behavioral cloning).
    \item \textbf{RL:} \textit{PPO-vanilla}~\cite{schulman2017ppo} (PPO from random initialization).
    \item \textbf{Graph-enhanced RL:} \textit{CSEAL}~\cite{liu2019cseal} (A2C with cognitive navigation), \textit{GEHRL}~\cite{li2023gehrl} (hierarchical RL with goal planning), \textit{DLELP}~\cite{cheng2025knowlp} (PPO with similarity agent for plateau detection).
    \item \textbf{LLM-enhanced:} \textit{KnowLP}~\cite{cheng2025knowlp} (DLELP with EDU-GraphRAG LLM-generated concept graph).
\end{itemize}

All baselines are re-implemented under identical experimental conditions (same DKT model, data split, and evaluation protocol) for fair comparison.

\subsection{Implementation Details}

\paragraph{KES.} We train a DKT model~\cite{piech2015dkt} with LSTM on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{Sim}}$ using the pyKT framework. For ASSISTments 2009, the DKT uses embedding size 600 and achieves AUC 0.83. For Junyi, embedding size 256 with AUC 0.74.

\paragraph{Concept Graph.} We construct the prerequisite and similarity graphs from the DKT's cross-concept influence matrix with thresholds $\tau = 0.25$ (ASSISTments) and $\tau = 0.05$ (Junyi).

\paragraph{Evolutionary Search.} Population size $N_{\text{pop}} = 60$, generations $N_{\text{gen}} = 50$, candidate cap $C = 30$, DPP diversity parameter $\lambda = 0.5$, $K = 5$ diverse paths per learner.

\paragraph{Policy Network.} 2-layer MLP with hidden size 512 (ASSISTments) or 256 (Junyi), ReLU activations, shared backbone with separate policy and value heads.

\paragraph{Training.} BC: 3,000 epochs, Adam with $\text{lr}=10^{-3}$. PPO: 30,000 episodes, batch size 128, $\text{lr}=10^{-4}$, $\epsilon=0.15$, entropy coefficient 0.03, cosine LR schedule, 4 PPO epochs per batch, gradient clip 0.5.

\section{Analysis}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Search Space Reduction and BC Initialization}

The central design principle of \ours{} is \emph{progressive search space reduction}. We first quantify this reduction, then explain three mechanisms through which it accelerates PPO.

The central advantage of \ours{} is the \emph{progressive reduction of the effective search space}. Consider the path space for $M=123$ concepts:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrl@{}}
\toprule
$L$ & $\binom{M}{L} \cdot L!$ & Magnitude \\
\midrule
5 & $2.5 \times 10^{10}$ & $10^{10}$ \\
10 & $2.3 \times 10^{20}$ & $10^{20}$ \\
20 & $4.7 \times 10^{38}$ & $10^{38}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
For $L=20$, a random policy's probability of finding a near-optimal path is vanishingly small. \ours{} addresses this through three compression steps: (1)~the candidate set $\mathcal{C}$ reduces the per-step action space from $M$ to $C \ll M$ using graph structure; (2)~evolution searches within this reduced space, finding high-quality paths in $O(N_{\text{pop}} \times N_{\text{gen}})$ evaluations; (3)~BC compresses $K \times N_{\text{train}}$ expert paths into a neural policy that generalizes to unseen learners. The result is that PPO starts from a policy whose support is concentrated on a \emph{polynomially-sized} subset of the exponential path space.

\paragraph{(1) Escaping the exploration bottleneck.} In LPR, the reward is \emph{sparse}---only the terminal EP at step $L$ provides a learning signal, while all intermediate steps receive zero reward. A randomly initialized policy produces near-random paths with near-zero EP, providing negligible gradient signal. The BC-initialized policy, by contrast, already produces paths with positive EP from the first episode, generating informative gradients from the start.

\paragraph{(2) Mitigating compounding errors.} Standard BC suffers from distribution shift~\cite{ross2011dagger}: small errors compound across sequential decisions because the policy encounters states not seen during training. In our framework, PPO \emph{corrects} this shift through online interaction---the fine-tuning stage explicitly trains on the policy's own state distribution, closing the gap that pure BC leaves open. This positions our approach between pure imitation (BC) and pure exploration (PPO), combining the sample efficiency of the former with the asymptotic optimality of the latter.

\paragraph{(3) Biasing exploration toward high-reward regions.} The entropy-regularized PPO objective encourages exploration, but the \emph{direction} of exploration depends on the initial policy. A BC-initialized policy explores \emph{around} known-good strategies, while a random policy explores uniformly. In the $\binom{M}{L}$ path space, the high-reward region is vanishingly small; BC initialization ensures that PPO's exploration budget is spent in the vicinity of this region rather than in the vast low-reward space.

\subsection{Role of DPP Diversity}

A critical but subtle design choice is the DPP-based diverse expert selection (Eq.~\ref{eq:dpp}). Without diversity enforcement, evolutionary search converges to a narrow fitness peak, producing near-identical expert paths. When BC trains on such redundant demonstrations, the resulting policy \emph{mode-collapses}---it memorizes a single strategy rather than learning a generalizable mapping from states to actions.

DPP selection ensures that the $K$ trajectories per learner cover qualitatively different strategies (e.g., prerequisite-first vs.\ target-direct vs.\ ZPD-focused paths). This diversity in the training set acts as implicit regularization, producing a BC policy that generalizes across the state space rather than overfitting to one behavioral mode. We validate this in the ablation study (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).

\section{Conclusion}
%% ============================================================

We presented \ours{}, a three-stage framework for learning path recommendation that bridges evolutionary search and reinforcement learning through behavioral cloning. By generating diverse expert trajectories via evolutionary algorithms and distilling them into a neural policy, \ours{} provides a strong initialization for PPO fine-tuning that overcomes the cold-start exploration problem. Extensive experiments on two real-world datasets demonstrate state-of-the-art performance and significantly faster convergence compared to existing methods.

\paragraph{Limitations and Future Work.}

\textit{Computational cost.} The evolutionary expert generation requires $N_{\text{train}} \times N_{\text{pop}} \times N_{\text{gen}} \times L$ KES evaluations as a preprocessing step. On ASSISTments 2009 (921 learners), this takes approximately 70 minutes on a single GPU. However, this cost is (a)~\emph{amortized}---experts are generated once and reused across all BC training runs---and (b)~\emph{parallelizable}---per-learner searches are independent and can be distributed across GPUs. For datasets with $>$10K learners (e.g., Junyi), we observe linear scaling at $\sim$1 learner/second with batched GPU evaluation.

\textit{Candidate space sensitivity.} The evolutionary candidate set $\mathcal{C}$ relies on the concept graph structure (prerequisites, similarity) and ZPD heuristics. While our DKT-derived graph is data-driven rather than manually constructed, the candidate cap $C$ and ZPD threshold are hyperparameters. We leave systematic sensitivity analysis for future work.

\textit{Future directions.} Extending \ours{} to multi-objective optimization (balancing mastery improvement with learner engagement and cognitive load), evaluating with human learners beyond simulation, and exploring online adaptation of the evolutionary experts as the policy improves are promising directions.
